# Music, Likeness, and Intellectual Property

The failure mode here is different from the rest: these problems usually surface *after* approval, as a muted ad, a takedown, or a rights-holder claim. Get them right during sourcing, because they're expensive to fix once the edit is locked.

## Music

**The default assumption to correct:** the trending song a brand wants is almost certainly unusable. TikTok's licensing deals with major labels cover organic personal use — they do not extend to commercial advertising. Using a track from the general music catalogue in a paid ad risks the ad being rejected, muted, or blocked.

Three legitimate routes:

1. **TikTok's Commercial Music Library (CML)** — the default. Over a million tracks pre-cleared by TikTok for commercial use, available to Business Account holders. Sourced largely from independent artists and production music houses, which is why mainstream chart hits and viral trending sounds are largely absent from it.
2. **A direct sync licence** from the rights holders — label for the recording, publisher for the composition. Both are needed. TikTok will require proof of the licence.
3. **Original or fully-owned music** — commissioned score, or a production-library track licensed for advertising with documentation retained.

Practical consequences worth telling the user early, because they change the creative:

- A trend-jacking concept built on a specific viral sound usually cannot survive contact with this rule. Find the hook in the *format* of the trend rather than the track.
- **Spark Ads inherit the problem.** Boosting an organic post that used a non-commercial track converts a permitted organic use into a commercial one. Check the sound before boosting.
- CML tracks tend to be shorter and structured differently from chart music. Cut to the track you actually have rather than editing to a temp and swapping later.

## Footage and stock assets

- **Use of clips from any unauthorized media sources to promote your product or service is prohibited** — film and TV clips, sports footage, news segments, other brands' ads, and other creators' videos without a licence.
- Stock footage, images, music, and fonts need a licence that covers *paid advertising*. Many "free" and personal-use licences explicitly exclude it, as do several standard stock licences without an extended or enhanced tier.
- AI-generated assets don't sidestep this: an AI render styled on protected IP is still an IP problem, and it triggers the AIGC disclosure requirement in `claims-and-copy.md`.
- Keep licence documentation per asset. If an ad is challenged, the ability to produce proof quickly is what keeps the campaign live.

## Third-party brands and trademarks

- Using third-party brands without authorization is prohibited.
- Displaying unauthorized third-party names, logos, or brands in a way that could mislead users about brand affiliation is prohibited.

Comparative advertising that names a competitor is a genuine legal grey area and varies by market; combined with the restrictions on comparative claims in `claims-and-copy.md`, naming a competitor on screen is rarely worth the risk. Incidental background logos in real-world footage are a lower but non-zero risk — blur or avoid them where practical.

## Likeness and public figures

- Using a public figure's likeness without permission is prohibited, and using it in a misleading context — implying an endorsement that doesn't exist — is specifically called out.
- This covers AI-generated or synthesized faces and voices of real people. A synthetic celebrity voiceover is a likeness violation and an undisclosed-AIGC violation at once.
- Everyone appearing on camera needs a release covering paid advertising use, in the markets where the ad will run, for the duration it will run.
- Creator content repurposed as an ad ("whitelisting") needs the creator's explicit agreement to paid usage — an organic collaboration does not grant it.
- Synthetic depictions of minors are prohibited outright.

## Counterfeit goods

Non-genuine products passed off as genuine, and replicas, imitations, or similar unauthorized items, cannot be promoted or sold. This includes "inspired by" and "dupe" framing where the creative leans on the original brand's identity to sell the copy — a dupe positioned on its own merits is different from one that uses a protected trade dress or name to trade on the original.

## TikTok's own brand assets

Ad content and landing pages must not display elements related to TikTok — the TikTok logo, TikTok UI, or copy like "TikTok Bestseller" — and must not imply sponsorship, endorsement, or partnership with TikTok or ByteDance without permission. See `creative-and-format.md`, where this overlaps with the ban on fake interactive elements.
